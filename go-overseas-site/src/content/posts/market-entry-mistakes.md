International expansion rarely fails in a single dramatic moment. It erodes — quietly,
expensively — through a handful of avoidable decisions. After dozens of cross-border
launches, the same patterns keep surfacing.

## 1. Treating the new market like home with a different flag

The product that won at home was shaped by a specific buyer, in a specific context, with
specific competitors. Drop it unchanged into a new market and you're betting that none of
that context mattered. It always does.

> The goal isn't translation. It's re-validation.

## 2. Hiring a "country manager" before you have a strategy

A great local hire can execute a plan. They cannot invent one from scratch while also being
your only person on the ground. Sequence matters: **strategy first, then the team to run it.**

## 3. Underestimating the regulatory runway

Licensing, tax registration, data residency, employment law — these are not paperwork. In
regulated sectors they *are* the timeline. We've seen launches slip two quarters because
licensing was treated as a formality rather than a critical path.

## 4. Spreading across three markets at once

Three half-funded entries almost always lose to one fully-committed one. Concentration beats
optionality in the early phase. Win one market, build the playbook, then replicate.

## 5. Declaring victory at first revenue

First revenue is a signal, not a finish line. The real question is whether the *unit
economics* hold once you're paying for local sales, support, and compliance. Many "successful"
launches are quietly unprofitable for years.

---

None of these are exotic. That's the point — expansion is rarely killed by the unknown. It's
killed by the familiar, repeated in an unfamiliar place.
